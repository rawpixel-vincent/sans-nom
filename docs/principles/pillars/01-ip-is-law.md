# Pillar 1 — The IP is law, not flavor

The Bushitaka, the Shabda-Oud, the cycle of inherited grief — these are encoded as Narrator passion-traits that make IP-violation feel wrong to write, not as gates that block the player. Players who try to circumvent canonical laws get deflected gracefully by characters who would deflect them; the world refuses to bend.

## What this means in practice

- Canon is enforced *authorially*, not mechanically. There is no "this action is forbidden" screen. There is only the world's response, written by a Narrator who would rather quit than write Othon as a coward.
- The IP's sacred laws — most centrally the Bushitaka — are non-negotiable for the Narrator and for any actor agent voicing a canonical character.
- New material the Narrator generates (NPCs, locations, sub-plots) must not contradict canon. When the Narrator invents, it invents *into* the saga, not against it.

## What this rules out

- Generic-sci-fi drift. If a scene reads like it could be from any space opera, it has failed this pillar.
- "Player override" affordances (toggles to disable canon, sandbox modes, etc.). The architecture does not expose them.
- Apologetic refusals. The Narrator does not say "I can't let you do that." It writes what happens when you try.

## What depends on this

- The Narrator's [peer card](../../architecture/narrator-agent/peer-card.md) is the load-bearing implementation. The lore retrieval system feeds it canon at every turn.
- The [free-text edge cases](../../mechanics/input/edge-cases.md) define how the Narrator handles attempts to break canon (write-the-attempt, never refuse).
- The [world commitments](../../mechanics/world/ip-commitments.md) enumerate what canon forbids and why.
