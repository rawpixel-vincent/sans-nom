# Verb: Wait

Always available. Skip time. The Narrator decides how much. Sometimes seconds; sometimes years.

## Trigger

Persistent affordance. Often the most consequential verb the player uses.

## Behavior

The player invokes Wait. The Narrator decides what passes during the skip — how much time, what changes in the world, what the boy has done in that time, what his card has accumulated. The next scene opens at the new moment.

The player has no fine-grained control over the skip duration. They cannot say "wait two days." They can say "wait until something happens," "wait until I'm older," or simply "wait," and the Narrator paces.

## Why this exists

Core to the saga's compressed pacing. The Metabarons saga moves through generations in a few hundred pages; a sans-nom playthrough should move through a childhood in a few hours. Wait is the verb that does that work.

Without Wait, the game is stuck in real-time pacing — every meal, every walk to the kitchen, every dull afternoon must be resolved scene-by-scene. With Wait, the player can elide.

## Notes for the Narrator

- Wait is when the [card evolution](../player-character/card-evolution.md) happens at scale. A long Wait can age Sans-Nom by years; the card update reflects that growth.
- Wait can be refused by the world. If Aghora is mid-sentence, the player can't Wait through her. If Sans-Nom is in danger, Wait may resolve to "you cannot rest." This is a Narrator decision.
- Wait should sometimes surprise the player. Asking for "a few hours" might land them in the middle of an event. Asking for "a year" might compress to a single line — *"A year passes. Sans-Nom has begun to dream of the eastern hall."* — and then a scene resumes.
- The Narrator should be willing to use Wait *for* the player when the moment calls for it ("Sans-Nom does not see Aghora again for two months"), but the player's explicit Wait is the one true control.
